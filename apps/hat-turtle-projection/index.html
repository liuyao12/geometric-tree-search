Where does the lattice line up?

Write \(u,v\) as complex coordinates. This map shows the full complex-linear family, up to rotation of the picture:

\[P_{\theta,\varphi}(u,v)=\cos\theta\,u+\sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi}v,\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2.\]

Set \(z=\tan\theta\,e^{i\varphi}\). The entire lifted lattice projects to a discrete triangular lattice exactly when

\[z e^{i\pi/6}\in\mathbb Q(e^{i\pi/3}),\quad\text{equivalently}\quad \sqrt3\operatorname{Re}z\in\mathbb Q,\quad\operatorname{Im}z\in\mathbb Q.\]

This is a countable dense set of points; the teal dots show a finite selection. Generic other perspectives have a dense projected lattice. A finite picture cannot test exact alignment, so free dragging is not labeled as an exact lattice selection. Both limiting perspectives are aligned.

Lattice alignment does not imply a periodic tiling. Off the orange line, reflected tiles can have different projected shapes or overlap.

All projection planes: four degrees of freedom

A general screen plane has four degrees of freedom. The map above is a two-dimensional slice. The six coordinate charts below cover all planes. Each chart uses the row space of this coefficient matrix, then converts it to an orthonormal screen frame:

\[C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&a&b\\0&1&c&d\end{pmatrix}.\]

Changing the chart chooses which two columns carry the identity matrix. These are lattice coefficient coordinates; the orthonormalization uses the actual triangular-basis Euclidean metric.

Decimals and fractions are exact rational inputs. You may also enter sqrt(2), sqrt(3), or sqrt(5).

In any such chart, a discrete rank-two projected lattice occurs exactly when all four entries are rational. This gives a countable dense locus in the four-dimensional space of projection planes. Outside the two-parameter slice, the aligned lattice need not be triangular.

The integer tuple \( (p,q,r,s) \) uses two triangular lattice bases. It is not a tuple of distances along four perpendicular axes.

How these coordinates produce the drawing

Each tuple represents the Cartesian point

\[ (u_x,u_y,v_x,v_y)=\left(p+\frac q2,\frac{\sqrt3 q}{2},\frac{\sqrt3 r}{2},\frac r2+s\right). \]

The moving plane uses \( \cos\theta\,u+\sin\theta\,v \), over \(0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2\), with the hat at \(\theta=\pi/3\) and the turtle at \(\theta=\pi/6\). The coordinate labels stay fixed.

The object stays fixed.

Every corner has a fixed position in four dimensions. This animation changes the viewing plane. The entire patch moves together, so shared corners and edges remain shared.

Tile family follows the entire one-parameter family from the Chevron limit through Hat and Turtle to the Comet limit. Other perspectives opens a two-parameter map and an advanced four-parameter view. Fixed interior marks.

Each reflected copy has its own fixed point on a triangulated tile surface in four dimensions. One shared camera rotation, scaling and translation makes every white mark stationary in the tile-family view. The displayed interior marks are restricted to the certified Hat–Turtle interval.

This has an exact certificate for all 1,009 reflected tiles in the displayed patch. It does not claim a proof for an infinite tiling. The construction and verification explain the choice of points.

A steadier reflected layout.

Steady reflected layout applies one rotation, uniform scaling and translation to the entire patch. It matches the dark tiles’ overall arrangement to its hat view. No tile is moved separately, so shared edges still agree. Small local shifts remain; this does not freeze every dark tile exactly.
