develop into the plane.

An analytic faithfulness theorem for the declared unmarked unit-rhomb sector model. The finite template hypotheses are exhaustively checked. The infinite argument is written mathematics, not a formally checked Hilbert certificate.

Statement and scope

Let \(S\) be a nonempty set of the declared thick and thin unit-rhomb placements: translations in \(\mathbb Z[\zeta_5]\), and the ten rotations generated by \(\eta=-\zeta_5^3\). At each named vertex, a rhomb occupies the consecutive angular sectors inside its corner, with \(t=1\) in those slots. Assume:

  1. All sector sums are capacity-legal.
  2. The placements are connected through shared named vertices.
  3. Every vertex of every placement has all ten sector sums equal to \(1\).

Then the developed polygons form an edge-to-edge tiling of \(\mathbb R^2\): their interiors are disjoint and their union is the entire plane. Conversely, any edge-to-edge plane tiling by these placements satisfies the sector and saturation conditions.

This gives a conditional equivalence for complete connected assignments. It does not turn a finite one-corona witness into an infinite extension, prove that a particular search trajectory continues, characterize Penrose arrows, or establish aperiodicity. It does not require every point of the dense module to be a tiling vertex. The turtle’s scalar angle weights do not supply the directional partition used here.

The historical pilot completes only the initial stars and leaves exposed obligations incomplete. Its saved “faithfulness open” status is historical; this theorem addresses that model gap under full saturation. Finite partial point patches still need separate polygon audits.

Sector saturation forces full edge partners

At a vertex \(v\), each corner occupies a consecutive nonempty interval of the ten-sector circle, of width \(1,2,3\) or \(4\). Capacity and saturation partition that circle. In particular, each endpoint ray of a corner is the start or end ray of exactly one adjacent corner on the opposite side.

All sides have length \(1\), and ray \(j\) has exact direction \(\eta^j\). Both adjacent faces therefore have the same whole edge, from \(v\) to \(v+\eta^j\), with opposite boundary orientations. A shorter edge, a gap, or a third partner cannot satisfy these assumptions. Applying the same reasoning at the other endpoint gives consistent edge gluing.

The finite catalog checks all 80 transformed corner identities, including both inventory aliases of each of 40 physical corners. All complete root-star partitions occur in the 708-star catalog. A separate enumeration by cyclic compositions verifies completeness of that catalog. There are 4,010 radial unit seams; all have exactly two opposite partners. Rotating all stars gives 7,080 checked configurations. Translations cancel from the edge differences, so the same reasoning applies at every named vertex.

The two sides opposite a rhomb corner are each at perpendicular distance at least \(\tfrac12\) from that corner, as the exact altitude checks verify. A full star therefore contains the open disk of radius \(\tfrac12\) around its root, assembled from disjoint angular wedges. This justifies the dashed local-chart circle in the interactive figure.

The abstract complex is a flat surface

Take disjoint copies of the rhomb faces. Glue matching whole edges by their unit parametrizations and identify equally named endpoints. This abstract complex does not assume that its developed polygon interiors are disjoint. An accidental crossing in the drawing does not itself glue faces.

Every edge has two incident faces. Every vertex link is the single cyclic partition of the direction circle, with total angle \(2\pi\). At most ten faces meet at a vertex. Thus face interiors, edge interiors and vertex stars each have a disk neighbourhood. Countably many placements and finite incidence give a locally finite, Hausdorff, second-countable surface \(X\) without boundary. Vertex connectivity also implies connectivity of \(X\), since each complete vertex star is connected through its edge partners.

On each face, use its given Euclidean coordinates. The gluings agree on endpoints and whole edges, yielding a developing map \(f:X\to\mathbb R^2\). The sector partition makes this map injective in a neighbourhood of each vertex; the two opposite faces give the same property at an edge. The map supplies flat coordinate charts with compatible Euclidean metrics. Consequently \(X\) is a connected flat Riemannian surface and \(f\) is a local isometry.

Local isometry alone is insufficient. The next step rules out a missing end reached after finite intrinsic travel.

A graph-distance barrier proves completeness

Barycentrically subdivide every rhomb: introduce its centre and edge midpoints, giving eight triangles per face. The resulting graph is connected and locally finite. Fix a graph vertex \(b_0\). At every graph vertex \(b\), set \(n(b)\) to its graph distance from \(b_0\), then extend \(n\) affinely over each triangle. Adjacent values differ by at most \(1\).

A barycentric triangle can be ordered as a rhomb corner \(a\), an adjacent edge midpoint \(m\), and the face centre \(c\). Put \(e_1=m-a\) and \(e_2=c-a\). Exact arithmetic verifies, for all 160 triangles of both prototypes and all ten rotations,

\[\lVert e_1\rVert=\tfrac12,\qquad \lVert e_2\rVert\le1,\qquad |\det(e_1,e_2)|\ge\tfrac18.\]

Write \(\alpha=n(m)-n(a)\) and \(\beta=n(c)-n(a)\). Both have absolute value at most \(1\). Solving the two equations for the affine gradient gives

\[\lVert\nabla n\rVert\le\frac{|\alpha|\lVert e_2\rVert+|\beta|\lVert e_1\rVert}{|\det(e_1,e_2)|}\le12.\]

The function agrees across triangle boundaries, so it is globally \(12\)-Lipschitz in the intrinsic length metric. This follows first along paths crossing finitely many triangles; compact path images meet finitely many cells by local finiteness. Therefore a point \(x\) at intrinsic distance at most \(R\) from \(b_0\) satisfies \(n(x)\le12R\).

All three vertex values of a triangle differ by at most \(1\). Every triangle meeting that metric ball consequently has its vertices in the finite graph ball of radius \(\lceil12R+1\rceil\). The metric ball lies in a finite union of compact triangles. It is closed in that compact subcomplex, hence compact. Every bounded closed ball is compact, so every Cauchy sequence converges: \(X\) is metrically complete.

This uses a uniform lower bound on triangle size, together with finite incidence. Dense physical coordinates do not invalidate it: the graph and metric in this step belong to the abstract glued surface.

Completeness turns development into plane coverage

A local isometry from a complete Riemannian manifold to a connected manifold of the same dimension is a covering map, including surjectivity. This is Urs Lang, Lecture Notes on Riemannian Geometry, Proposition 4.9, page 50 (June 16, 2020).

Apply it to \(f:X\to\mathbb R^2\). The plane is simply connected; its connected covering has one sheet. Hence \(f\) is bijective and, as a local isometry, a global isometry. Distinct abstract face interiors have distinct images, and every point of the plane has a preimage. These are exactly polygon non-overlap and whole-plane coverage. Boundary identifications are the declared whole edges and vertices, so the tiling is edge-to-edge.

The converse is immediate from an edge-to-edge tiling: its corner wedges partition the full direction circle at each tiling vertex. This establishes the stated equivalence.

Why connectivity must be explicit

The analytic comparison control uses a periodic thick-rhomb grid with basis \(1,\zeta_5\). Its vertex set is \(\Lambda=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\zeta_5\); four grid faces complete the star at each vertex. Translate a second entire grid by \(a=\varphi^{-1}=(-1,0,-1,-1)\) in the rank-four basis. The last two coefficients show \(a\notin\Lambda\), so \(\Lambda\) and \(a+\Lambda\) are disjoint.

Both infinite layers separately saturate every generated vertex, and their point supports never meet. Their union is capacity-legal but geometrically overlaps everywhere. It has two vertex-connected components. The theorem applies to each component separately, not to their overlaid union. These known analytic grids are comparison controls only; they never enter discovery or policy training.

Each grid’s plane coverage follows directly from the nonsingular parallelogram basis and the translated fundamental cells. This control is not a newly discovered Penrose construction.
