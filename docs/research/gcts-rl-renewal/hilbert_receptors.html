The assertion and its assumptions

Work in one-sorted first-order logic with equality and predicates \(\operatorname{Point}\), \(\operatorname{Line}\) and \(\operatorname{Incident}\). The chosen fragment is the guarded planar uniqueness axiom corresponding to Hilbert I.2; this does not include his full geometric foundation.

For any points \(A\) and \(B\) and lines \(u\) and \(v\), the following nine conditions force \(u=v\):

  • \(\operatorname{Point}(A)\), \(\operatorname{Point}(B)\), \(\operatorname{Line}(u)\), \(\operatorname{Line}(v)\).
  • \(\neg(A=B)\).
  • \(\operatorname{Incident}(A,u)\), \(\operatorname{Incident}(B,u)\), \(\operatorname{Incident}(A,v)\), \(\operatorname{Incident}(B,v)\).

The proof reader abbreviates this conjunction as \(H\). Given \(H\) and \(\operatorname{Incident}(C,u)\), search seeks \(\operatorname{Incident}(C,v)\). The names in the measured assertion are arbitrary constants, not coordinates. The theorem is a consequence under these hypotheses; a fully universally closed conditional is a further certificate-construction task. No hypothesis \(\operatorname{Point}(C)\) is needed for equality substitution.

The source is Hilbert’s 1903 axioms, with Wilkins’s parallel translation, Group I. The classic statement that distinct lines share at most one point remains an explicit next target for this unchanged-palette search.
