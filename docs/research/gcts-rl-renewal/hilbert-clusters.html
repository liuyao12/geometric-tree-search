One expressive base inventory, shared by arithmetic and geometry.

The user’s chosen target is a single tile system for first-order proof search. Its explicit operations should cover formula syntax, conjunction, disjunction, implication, negation, equality, substitution, universal and existential quantification, and variable scope. Readable inference tiles are preferable to minimizing tile count. Arithmetic and geometry differ in their encoded axiom declarations; they do not receive different base logical inventories.

\[\text{axiom data} + \text{statement} + \text{one fixed inventory}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{search}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{checked certificate}.\]

For a truly finite uniform set, formulas and variable names must be encoded as strings over a fixed alphabet. Treating each whole formula as a new blue color would make the inventory depend on the query. Recognizable axiom schemas, including arithmetic induction, must be encoded and checked rather than importing an unchecked inference. The first implementation gate is the same unchanged inventory solving both an arithmetic statement and a Hilbert incidence statement from different encoded inputs.

Current status: The unchanged first-order checker is shared. The producers still compile separate bounded, goal-derived inventories with whole-formula IDs and positional reference contacts. This is useful proof-search evidence, but it does not yet meet the fixed-inventory gate. A sound compiler and a fair search across increasing proof bounds remain required.

Clusters enter the reusable inventory after their assumptions, conclusions, scope conditions and expansion are checked. Larger clusters can compose smaller ones. “Lemma” and “theorem” describe their mathematical use, rather than a size threshold. Reuse avoids rediscovery of an internal derivation; matching, storage, validation and search still cost time. A learned cluster is an additional checked option, and all base alternatives remain available.

A family takes its shape from matching receptors.

A receptor is a typed proof obligation: formula structure, variable bindings, context and scope conditions. A family binds its incoming receptors to available facts, instantiates a checked derivation, and exposes outgoing receptors for later tiles or clusters. Formula binding and the internal dependency graph determine a valid instance; positions may change while proof direction and exact point agreement are preserved.

\[\begin{gathered}\Gamma\Rightarrow\exists x\,P(x),\qquad\forall x\,((\Gamma\land P(x))\Rightarrow Q)\\[2pt]\longmapsto\quad\Gamma\Rightarrow Q,\qquad x\notin\operatorname{FV}(\Gamma)\cup\operatorname{FV}(Q).\end{gathered}\]

This example interface discharges a witness without allowing it to escape. Its context and binder conditions are part of the family’s type. The next family can consume its conclusion. The incoming facts may be separated by other proof cells, so marking contacts can extend beyond adjacency.

Design sketch: the same typed interface with different placements and reach. This illustration is not an additional search result. The measured families below vary formulas and translate learned offsets; receptor-dependent deformation and recursive family composition have not yet been evaluated.

The next experiment will remove fixed offset distances from a learned dependency template, bind receptors by exact syntax and scope checks, and enumerate admissible layouts. It will compare fixed clusters, receptor families, classical unification-based reuse, and no reuse. RL may propose useful instances and new compositions. Independent checking determines admissibility; learned preference alone cannot justify pruning.

Aperiodicity is not the universality criterion.

Wang introduced the domino framework in his 1961 paper on mechanical theorem proving. Berger’s domino-problem construction, described by its original AMS publisher, encodes machines whose nonhalting corresponds to plane tileability. The existence of aperiodic sets and the undecidability of the general domino problem are related results; choosing an arbitrary aperiodic set does not supply a universal interpreter.

For proof search, we need an explicit computation encoding and a decoder. One fixed universal proof-checking machine can, in principle, be represented by fixed translation-only tiles; the theory, statement and proposed certificate are encoded inputs. A finite region with suitable input and accepting boundaries represents a finite accepting run. A separate whole-plane nonhalting reduction has different boundary and result semantics.

\[\exists\pi\;\operatorname{Check}(A,\varphi,\pi)=\operatorname{accept}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists\text{ finite accepting tile patch for }(A,\varphi).\]

This equivalence is a required soundness-and-completeness theorem for the proposed encoding, not a theorem established by our present experiments. It expresses finite derivability in an effectively specified formal system, not all semantic truth. Universality gives expressive power; it gives no practical search speed guarantee. Aperiodicity is optional for this finite proof-patch route. No universal or aperiodic base tile set was chosen for the present notebooks.
