From an empty theorem library to infinitely many primes

In one continuous run, the search proved that every natural number greater than one has a prime divisor, then reused that checked theorem to show there is a prime above every bound.

The search proved \(\forall n>1,\ \exists p\,(\operatorname{Prime}(p)\land p\mid n)\) by strong induction. Its auxiliary grammar generated the claim that each bound \(n\) admits a positive common multiple of the integers from \(1\) through \(n\). In the induction step it found \(m(n+1)\), with \(m\) supplied by the induction hypothesis.

For infinitude, take a positive common multiple \(m\) up to the bound \(n\). A prime divisor of \(m+1\) cannot be at most \(n\): it would then divide both \(m\) and \(m+1\), and cancellation would make it divide \(1\). The checker confirmed that the saved proof uses both earlier theorems, yielding \(\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\ \exists p\,(\operatorname{Prime}(p)\land n<p)\).

What the run was given

The fixed boundary supplied 19 elementary arithmetic laws, including the primality case split and general divisibility cancellation under addition. Ordinary and strong induction were available as inference schemas. Terms have fixed lattice addresses, and compatible marks are joined by unification. The search uses fixed clause priorities and a finite grammar for auxiliary goals and witness terms. This experiment starts from the declared arithmetic laws and tests theorem reuse; it does not measure an RL speedup.
