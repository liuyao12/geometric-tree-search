The declared system

Use \(L=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb Z^3:x+y+z=0\}\), with first-two-component coordinates \((x,y)\). The Euclidean embedding is \(E(x,y)=(x+y/2,\sqrt3\,y/2)\); a shortest lattice edge has length \(1\). Let \(G\) consist of coordinate permutations with simultaneous sign \(1\) or \(-1\). These are exactly the twelve transformations of the frozen search model.

Let \(P_c\) be a closed turtle polygon in the declared inventory and let \(\hat t_c:L\to\{0,\ldots,12\}\) be its integer point occupancy from turtle.py. Each transformed placement has one owner identity. For a set \(S\) of distinct placements put

\[P(S)=\bigcup_{c\in S}P_c,\qquad \hat T_S(p)=\sum_{c\in S}\hat t_c(p).\]

Even an infinite placement set is locally finite: the inventory has finitely many bounded outlines and a discrete translation lattice. A bounded part of the plane can meet only finitely many such placements. Consequently the sums at points and the arguments about individual cells involve finite sets.

Cells belonging to lattice points

The elementary triangular grid has faces with vertices \((i,j),(i+1,j),(i,j+1)\) and \((i+1,j+1),(i,j+1),(i+1,j)\). Subdivide each face at its edge midpoints and barycenter. The resulting six flag triangles partition that face. A flag has one original lattice vertex, its owner.

\[d_0=(1,0),\ d_1=(0,1),\ d_2=(-1,1),\ d_3=(-1,0),\ d_4=(0,-1),\ d_5=(1,-1).\]

For a lattice point \(p\), the twelve flags it owns are

\[F_{p,i,d}=\operatorname{conv}\!\left(p,\ p+\frac d2,\ p+\frac{d_i+d_{i+1}}3\right),\qquad i\in\{0,\ldots,5\},\ d\in\{d_i,d_{i+1}\},\]

where the index is read modulo \(6\). Define its closed barycentric cell \(C_p\) to be the union of these twelve flags, and for a point set \(Q\) define \(\Omega_Q=\bigcup_{p\in Q}C_p\). Distinct flag interiors, and distinct cell interiors, are disjoint. All cells together cover the plane. A flag has coordinate area \(1/12\); a cell has coordinate area \(1\), or Euclidean area \(\sqrt3/2\).

Theorem

For this declared turtle system:

  1. \(\hat T_S(p)\le12\) for every \(p\in L\) if and only if all distinct polygons in \(S\) have disjoint interiors.
  2. For a capacity-legal \(S\), saturation of every point in \(Q\) is exactly continuous coverage of its cells: \[\bigl(\forall p\in Q:\hat T_S(p)=12\bigr)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Omega_Q\subseteq P(S).\]
  3. Containment of every tile's positive point support in \(A\) is exactly containment of those polygons in \(\Omega_A\). Therefore, with no separate exterior, a finite required set and allowed envelope certify \[\Omega_Q\subseteq P(S)\subseteq\Omega_A.\] If \(Q=A\), this is exact tiling of \(\Omega_A\).

In particular, legal saturation of all \(L\) is equivalent to a polygon tiling of the whole plane by the declared inventory. This equivalence supplies an interpretation of a hypothetical complete assignment. It supplies neither its existence nor a compatible infinite continuation of our finite patches.

Proof from independently checked finite hypotheses

Every polygon is a union of flags

The prototype contains exactly \(240\) complete flags. At each lattice point, their number equals \(\hat t(p)\), including boundary and interior points. The producer clips every potentially intersecting flag against an exact rational ear triangulation; each intersection is empty or the whole flag, and their areas sum to the polygon area. The independent auditor instead constructs flags face by face, classifies their centers by an exact ray test, cancels oriented internal edges, and checks that the remaining segments partition every polygon side exactly, with no extra boundary or hole.

The twelve symmetries preserve the triangular flag grid. The producer checks this action and transfers the prototype; the auditor reconstructs and verifies each transformed outline separately. Translations preserve flags and their owners. Thus every allowed polygon is a union of complete flags, and \(\hat t_c(p)\) counts its flags owned by \(p\).

Two polygons overlap exactly when the point model conflicts

Fix one prototype. For each other orientation, every translation whose polygon can intersect its bounding box lies in the inclusive integer rectangle

\[\min_x P-\max_x(gP)\le a\le\max_x P-\min_x(gP),\qquad \min_y P-\max_y(gP)\le b\le\max_y P-\min_y(gP).\]

There are \(2443\) distinct-owner cases after excluding the prototype's identical placement. All \(1047\) positive-area overlaps have a point with summed occupancy greater than \(12\), and all \(1396\) non-overlaps have no such point. Among the latter, \(304\) share positive-support points. The producer detects shared complete flags. The independent auditor uses rational horizontal slices in every band determined by the two polygons' vertices and segment intersections, checks their open interval intersections and produces an interior witness when they overlap. This includes narrow overlaps that a single band midpoint without intersection heights could miss.

Outside the rectangle, bounding boxes and positive point supports are separated. Applying the inverse transform of an arbitrary first placement reduces any allowed pair to a checked case or a separated pair. The transformations form a group and preserve the lattice and point sums. The pair statement therefore holds for every allowed pair, beyond the finite rectangle.

Disjoint flags turn scalar counts into coverage

If a full assignment obeys capacity, every pair does, by nonnegativity; the pair statement makes all polygon interiors disjoint. Conversely, disjoint polygon interiors give disjoint occupied flag interiors. At any point at most its twelve flags are occupied, so \(\hat T_S(p)\le12\). This proves the first claim.

In a legal assignment the sum at \(p\) counts distinct occupied flags among its twelve possible flags. It equals \(12\) exactly when all are occupied. An absent flag's open interior cannot be covered by flags belonging to other points. Thus its entire cell is covered exactly when that point is saturated. Taking unions proves the second claim.

A tile's positive support consists precisely of the owners of its occupied flags. Those flags all belong to \(\Omega_A\) exactly when those owners belong to \(A\), since distinct cell interiors are disjoint. Taking closed unions proves containment and the third claim. Choosing \(Q=L\) gives plane coverage, and choosing \(Q=A\) gives equality of the finite region boundaries. No geometric check has been inserted into the search algorithm.

What this changes for practical boundaries

The notebook's required points specify a continuous mandatory region \(\Omega_Q\), while its allowed envelope specifies \(\Omega_A\). A returned patch may extend beyond the required region. An unrequired annulus interior remains available for placements; a forbidden physical hole needs an appropriate allowed-envelope exclusion. An arbitrary prescribed continuous curve is not automatically a union of these cells. Exact authoring of other boundary shapes, or a stated approximation, remains separate work.

Fixed exterior contributions have a polygon interpretation only when they are realized by declared exterior placements and agree with their full point sums. All \(70\) completed notebook 21 evaluation and movable-family requests meet that condition. Their polygons cover all \(129360\) required flags across these requests, without duplicate occupied flags or flags outside the recorded envelopes. The experiment and its policy are explicitly reused; this is stronger verification of those results, without a new solving benchmark.

Compatible cluster expansions inherit the theorem: their aggregate point values count the union of their base flags. The earlier capacity-input law therefore guarantees polygon compatibility when the incoming occupancy is itself realized by a legal set of declared tiles. An arbitrary scalar trace need not have such a realization. Additional equality markings still impose their own independent restrictions.

The turtle has coordinate area \(20\), or Euclidean area \(10\sqrt3\). A finite closed cell target with zero exterior must therefore satisfy \(|A|=20|S|\). The closed radius-five hexagon has \(91\) points and fails this necessary area condition, yielding a simple analytic obstruction without search. Divisibility alone does not prove tileability.
