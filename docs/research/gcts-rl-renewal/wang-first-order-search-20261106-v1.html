Searching first-order proofs for a fixed Wang palette

The search now resolves clauses with variables directly. It can use disjunctive hypotheses, merge unifiable literals, and find deep term substitutions as part of the proof. After search, a compiler produces the ordinary ground proof rows checked by the same translation-only Wang palette.

Eleven theorems yielded accepting rectangles. They include Peano successor, Hilbert-style incidence uniqueness, a proof by cases, and two TPTP tutorial examples. The reader lets you follow both the clauses with variables and their expanded Wang rows.

Choose substitutions during search

The input is a closed first-order statement with typed symbols and explicit assumptions. The front end negates the goal, introduces Skolem symbols for the resulting existential quantifiers, and produces clauses. It includes the ordinary equality axioms and congruence schemas when the statement uses equality.

Binary resolution standardizes the parent variables apart and unifies complementary literals. Factoring unifies two same-sign literals in one clause and merges them. The unifier rejects a cyclic binding such as \(x\mapsto f(x)\). A predicate index and a shallow function-head check serve every search arm. Every fourth selection uses the oldest waiting clause. The experiments stop after 20,000 inference proposals or 18,000 stored clauses.

The compiler walks backwards from the empty clause and instantiates only the proof dependencies. A factoring step can collapse two identical ground literals into a single set member, requiring no separate tape instruction. The final ground certificate contains only schema instances and binary resolution. Independent replay checks the lifted substitutions, the ground certificate and the colored squares. The discovery algorithm itself runs on symbolic clauses.

Fair first-order resolution with factoring underlies an unbounded search for refutations. The experiments use bounded search and bounded clausification. Robinson’s resolution paper
